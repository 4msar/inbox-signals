The five, if you only read five

ranked

Ranked picks for an engineering lead at a software consultancy: pieces that change how you advise a client on AI-assisted review, runtime cost and delivery.

1
Dev InterruptedRead

Code review has become a stage: the job is now to classify, route and rebalance review

In LinearB’s benchmark of 8.1M PRs, AI-generated PRs merge at 32.7% against 84.4% for human PRs, and agentic PRs wait 5.3 times longer for a reviewer. Cate Huston found that 71% of issues flagged by automated checks never came up in human review. The author recommends risk-tier classification and promoting agents slowly. LinearB’s figures are sponsor data.

2
The Pragmatic EngineerRead

incident.io cut AI spend per developer from $4,500 to $1,600 a month while shipping more PRs

Spend runs near $1.6M for the year, peaking above $300K in July, roughly 80% Claude and 20% Cursor. PRs with no human reviewer rose from 12% to 43%, and incidents per 1,000 PRs fell 60% even though total incidents rose. Median engineer throughput went from 10.1 to 10.7 PRs a week, and tenured engineers from 11.1 to 19. Orosz discloses he is an incident.io investor.

3
The AI CornerRead

Buy hosted agents; build only the rules file and the gates

The newsletter cites Bloomberg on Uber, where more than 70% of PRs come from agents and review time rose from about 3 hours in 2024 to about 9 hours in 2026. Faros AI puts median PR review time up 441.5%. Meta’s Rule of Two allows at most two of untrusted input, sensitive access, and change or send. The Uber figures are second-hand.

4
ByteByteGoRead

LinkedIn’s AI reviewer runs parallel subagents across 40,000+ PRs a week

The final design uses independent parallel subagents, a merge step, a verification step, and layered rules for company, repo and PR. Engineers accepted 63.9% of its comments. Acceptance reached 100% for concurrency bugs and dead code, and 80% for logic errors. The vendor and single-model reviewers were dropped for noise and hallucinated line numbers.

5
Tech World With MilanRead

A three-month rewrite took nine months, and Brooks’s Law explains why

Milanović describes a rewrite that hit 140 special cases, added five engineers and ten agents, built 40 microservices, and broke the biggest customer’s import in month nine. Brooks’s Law gives 45 communication paths at 10 people and 105 at 15. His fixes: scope to one country first, agree API contracts before agents write code, and pair every metric with a counter-metric.

Engineering leadership

2 items

Managers, staff engineers and architects: decisions, bottlenecks and working with AI.

The Engineering Manager (James Stanier)Read

Most decisions are two-way doors, and treating them as one-way kills velocity

Stanier cites Bezos’s Type 1 and Type 2 framing and warns about one-way doors disguised as two-way ones, such as public APIs, data models and customer commitments. He cites a $99 course that took two months to approve and a code review that sat for three weeks. His remedies: answer escalations within about 24 hours, decide at about 70% of the information, and classify your last five decisions.

Software Architecture Guild (Ilya Hardzeenka)Read

Architects become the bottleneck when teams learn that asking the architect is the safe route

Each request seems reasonable, but together they form an unofficial architecture gate. The fix is explicit decision boundaries: a team may pick a library from an agreed set alone, while a shared-interface change needs the affected teams. The post has no quantitative data and opens with course-pitch links.

Essays & arguments

6 items

Long-form arguments that are not primarily news.

The Pragmatic EngineerPaywallRead

Correctness comes from types and realistic workloads, not from more unit tests

Sean Allen of Antithesis argues that sum types with exhaustive matching turn a forgotten case into a compile error. He favours workloads such as the Bank Test, where $10,000 moves between ten accounts and the total must always read $10,000, over unit tests. The preview cuts off before the supply-chain section.

Good at Business (Carly Taylor)Read

Managed personal agents hand too much power to firms that may not be trustworthy

Grok Bot launched at $300 a month, then dropped to $30, and its quotas were halved. Anthropic says agents cost four to fifteen times a normal chat. Taylor argues sites will block agents because they skip ads. Her examples are anecdotal.

The Founders CornerPaywallPromoRead

About one in four two-founder teams lost a co-founder within four years

Carta tracked 13,133 founders from US two-founder teams (2015–2024), and puts the 2017 cohort’s departure rate at 24.1%. In the worked example, a co-founder who leaves at month 13 under a four-year vest keeps 18.1% of the equity, and keeps 45% with no vesting. Equal splits rose from 31.5% to 45.9% over the period.

The Founders CornerPaywallPromoRead

Two $15K hires turn a default-alive startup into a default-dead one by month 16

The example company has $2M cash, $170K monthly expenses and $40K monthly revenue growing 7% a month, giving 15.4 months of runway. Revenue overtakes expenses in month 22, so it is default alive, but two $15K hires make it default dead, and growth would need to reach 8.1%. Carta puts the median seed-to-Series-A gap at 618 days.

The Beautiful Mess (John Cutler)Read

People read the same leadership statement as either an audit or an aspiration

Some people compare what leaders say with actual incentives and feel uncomfortable. Others hear the same words as aspirational. Cutler traces both reactions to early experience, and warns against turning a guess about someone’s motives into a verdict on their character. No numbers.

Refactoring (Luca Rossi)PromoRead

The gap between elite and median teams keeps widening: 8x, then 9x, then 10x

Quarterly research shows elite teams’ throughput advantage grew from 8x in Q1 to 9x in Q2 and 10x in Q3. Rossi attributes this to AI benefiting teams that already have good practices. The issue is mostly an announcement of the newsletter’s next stage.

Dev, DevOps & tooling

10 items

Runtime and build decisions, security findings, and the agent-era practices that come with them.

TLDR DevRead

Resend moved its email API from Node to Go in seven weeks

At a 50% rollout, median latency fell from 58 ms to 31 ms and memory per task from about 1.3 GB to 180 MB. Burst capacity and storage timeouts still needed tuning. The numbers are Resend’s own.

TLDR DevOpsRead

Cloudflare’s targeted DNS zone builds cut peak memory from 79 GiB to 12 GiB

The same change took the median build from 7.7 seconds to 3.3 seconds. These are Cloudflare’s own measurements.

TLDR DevOps / WizRead

Apache HTTP Server has a remote-code-execution race; upgrade to 2.4.71 or disable HTTP/2

The flaw is a race in assign_headers() in the HTTP/2 module. Wiz reports that 64% of cloud environments have vulnerable resources. Until you can upgrade, turning HTTP/2 off closes the exposure.

ByteByteGoRead

GitHub used Copilot agents to port the Copilot runtime from TypeScript to Rust

The body says 430K lines of TypeScript moved, at about $120K of AI tokens (136B tokens); the intro says nearly 800K lines. One agent split a 30K-line file across 15 child sessions. Latency fell from 5.25 s to 292 ms and memory from 1,383 MB to 126 MB. The lessons: strong end-to-end tests, and port first without redesigning.

TLDR DevOpsRead

An independent threat model found 30 Linkerd threats, and only one was a software bug

28 of the 30 threats are rated medium or high, and most come from default and configuration choices. The one software bug was fixed in February 2025.

TLDR DevRead

X rebuilt its web app as one server, in under six months

One Fastify and React app with streaming SSR replaces three servers. On a slow connection, the first profile post appears in 2.7 seconds, against more than 22 seconds before. The first version was built with AI coding agents.

TLDR AIRead

Google’s Gemini Decisions API evaluates up to 1,000 yes/no or rubric questions in one pass

The newsletter gives the price as $0.10 per two million input tokens, with outputs free. The price is as reported in the newsletter; check it against Google’s pricing page before relying on it.

TLDR DevRead

Dropbox’s scoped agent tasks delivered 22 accessibility fixes and cleared 53 test cleanups in a week

Engineers reviewed the agent output. This is the company’s own account of a one-week sprint, with no baseline comparison.

TLDR DevPromoRead

NVIDIA’s NeMo case: malformed agent tool calls fell from 142 to zero at Dassault

The evaluation score rose 28% and each run made 35% fewer tool calls, according to NVIDIA’s case study. This is single-customer vendor data.

TLDR AIRead

No model escaped the VM-host boundary in Perplexity’s SPACE tests, but several bypassed network controls

Twelve models were tested against an agent sandbox. Several got around network controls through DNS spoofing and shared-IP exploits before fixes, and capture-the-flag prompts distracted agents from the boundary under test.

System design

6 items

Patterns and trade-offs, mostly from System Design Nuggets, Neo Kim and ByteByteGo.

System Design Newsletter (Neo Kim)Read

Agent debugging needs OpenTelemetry traces, and the key metric is cost per successful task

The guest post treats the whole agent harness (reasoning loop, memory, evals, guardrails and human-in-the-loop) as the unit to trace. Its worked sizing example is five AKS pods at 2 vCPU and 6 GB each, or 10 vCPU and 30 GB in total. Mostly conceptual.

ByteByteGo RoundupRead

Agents opened more than 40% of GitHub PRs in September, up from 17% in March

The figure comes from GitHub’s Octoverse, as relayed by ByteByteGo, which says more than 40% of September PRs started with agents. The same roundup says Anthropic moved from Git clones to a server-side service, Scion. Some sentences in the roundup are the author’s own interpretation.

System Design NuggetsPaywallRead (paywalled preview)

Partial failure: losing 2 of 10 cache nodes can double database load and cascade

The preview covers partial failure, split-brain and cascading failure, with defences such as idempotency keys, the outbox pattern, sagas, quorums, fencing tokens and circuit breakers. Its cache example is the cascade. The post is paywalled after scenario three.

System Design NuggetsPaywallRead (paywalled preview)

Idempotency keys alone don’t give exactly-once payments

The post treats exactly-once as a semantic achieved with three patterns: idempotency keys, deduplication and a transactional outbox. It walks through four failure cases, including a lost response, a crash after a database write and concurrent retries. The mechanisms are paywalled after the key-table section.

System Design NuggetsPaywallRead (paywalled preview)

Deploying is not releasing: blast radius, detection and rollback decide the pattern

The post frames the choice around three questions. The preview covers recreate, rolling and blue-green deployments. Canary releases, which start at about 1% of traffic, are in the paywalled part.

System Design Newsletter (Neo Kim)PaywallRead (paywalled preview)

Continuous batching and PagedAttention are the main levers for LLM inference cost

Iteration-level scheduling, from Orca and vLLM, lets batches refill as requests finish. The vLLM paper reported near-zero KV-cache waste and 2 to 4 times the throughput, and FlashAttention computes exact attention with linear auxiliary memory. The GPU-kernels section is paywalled.
