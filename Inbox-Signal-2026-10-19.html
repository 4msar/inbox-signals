The five, if you only read five

ranked

Picked for an engineering lead at a software consultancy across the week's 198 emails: pieces that change how you would advise a client on AI-assisted delivery, review, governance and cost, not just what launched.

1
Tech World with Milan (Trends #12)Read

Teams ship about 3x more code and still review it the same way: output is up, measured value and trust are not

Linear's report (199,000 users, June 2024 – August 2026) has agent-using teams going from 21 to 65 PRs a week against 8 to 10 for others, yet nobody saved time; Linear's conclusion is that deciding what to build, not execution, is now the limit. METR had four maintainers (scikit-learn, Sphinx, pytest) review 296 SWE-bench-passing AI PRs: merge rates ran 24 points below the grader's scores once adjusted for how strict the maintainers are, and the same maintainers merged only 68% of the original human patches.

Sonar says AI writes 42% of committed code, 96% of developers don't trust it, and only 48% always check it. DORA's ROI view: 35-40% gains on greenfield work, 10% or less on legacy code. Several figures are second-hand summaries of other reports, and the cause given for the 8-hour GitHub outage is Milan's own summary. Free, full text.

2
Anthropic engineering (via TLDR, TLDR IT, Meng Li)Scion postCode review postMeng Li on review

Anthropic left Git because agent-driven merges outgrew it, and rebuilt code review around risk tiers rather than reading every diff

Merges averaged about 2,500 a day in May and passed 30,000 a day by September, so a seven-person team (per Meng Li) built a replacement, Scion, and moved the monorepo over in five months. Reported results (TLDR Dev and Rohit): 200x less CPU and memory than the old Git-mirroring setup and 25% of CI CPU freed (TLDR Dev). Rohit notes the post itself says Git remains right for most repos.

The Claude Code team's post says continuous review, automated verification, multi-layer checks and a tiered risk system for approvals now sit in front of any human reviewer. Meng Li's teaser adds that about 90% of Claude Code's code is written by Claude, merged PRs more than doubled from June to September and the share needing rollback fell by more than 40%. His four rules for AI-written PRs: the opener owns it, add a 'How was this verified?' field, ask different review questions, and only the code owner merges. The three practices he would copy are not named in the preview.

3
The Pragmatic Engineer (podcast)Read

Uber's AI developer infrastructure: a model gateway, tiered spend controls, and code review as the new bottleneck

Distinguished Engineer Uday Kiran Medisetty says Uber had 2 services in 2015, thousands by 2019 and about the same now, though the business is roughly 10x larger. Its Model Gateway (2023) keeps tooling model- and harness-agnostic, with AI Guard, per-user spend tracking and audit logs, and engineers can switch between Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode. Spend is allocated in tiers with manager sign-off to exceed it; token costs fell about 50% in recent months.

All PRs are still human-reviewed, but review is now the bottleneck, so AI review, screenshots/videos and risk scores come first. CI jobs more than doubled in 12 months, forcing an internal 'code red', and PRs are now tagged by type rather than tracked as 'PR velocity'. Podcast summary; a useful template for client governance conversations.

TLDR Dev / TLDR ITCode is a commodityDeployCos

'Code is a commodity now' — and AI labs, hyperscalers and consultancies are building deployment companies to sell what is left

The efp.asia essay says agencies should shift from selling hours to domain expertise, product judgment and outcome accountability, with smaller generalist teams; it admits the productivity evidence is mixed and verification is costly in mature codebases. TLDR Dev's blurb is all the email gives on it.

'The rise of DeployCos' (TLDR IT) describes labs, hyperscalers and consultancies forming companies that embed forward-deployed engineers in enterprises to close the 'diffusion gap': workflow integration, systems access and change management. Neither blurb has numbers and both are opinion, but together they describe your direct competition and the opening for a consultancy.

TLDR AI / TLDR IT / The AI Cornerpaywalled previewAnthropic researchAI Corner breakdown

Anthropic's own workforce study: agents now outwork people inside the company, and junior technical hiring has collapsed

By August agents worked about 6 hours for every human hour in employee-directed sessions and 19x in research orgs, and commits per engineer were 5.1x their December 2025 level (The AI Corner). The junior share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9% since early 2025; TLDR AI and The AI Corner agree on that figure. Agents sit idle about 80% of an interactive session waiting for a human, so human attention is the bottleneck.

Caveats: it is Anthropic's own data (HR records, telemetry and 17 interviews), and the AI Corner author discounts the 5.1x commits figure against an outside study of 500,000+ developers where commits rose about 240% but releases only about 30%. The email also prints a weekly reading load that exceeds the hours in a week, so that figure is left out. The workbook and prompts are paywalled.

Latent.Space (AIE NYC keynotes)Read

Safe agent autonomy depends on where controls sit, not how many restrictions you add

Two Sigma's John Feminella attacks 'approval fatigue': approving everything blocks work and adds risk once attention lapses, so use composable controls, with classifiers for ambiguous calls and deterministic rules for clear ones ('Why do cars have brakes? So that you can go fast and stop safely'). Bloomberg's Andrey Rybka argues for deterministic controls outside the model: scoped credentials, tool-call controls, outbound traffic limits, audit trails, budgets and kill switches.

Anthropic's Keon Shahab describes a trust layer of identity, sandbox and approval before action; OpenAI's Joseph Kim predicts finance agents doing idea generation and trading in 2027. These are vendor presentations, and there are no figures.

ByteByteGovendor claimRead

A small energy-based model solved 96.2% of Sudoku puzzles where a panel of frontier LLMs managed 2%

In Logical Intelligence's public experiment, a panel of frontier LLMs solved 2% of puzzles at about $11,000 of API calls and the best single LLM solved 6.9%. All results are company-reported and come from a guest interview, so treat them as self-reported.

TLDR DevHuman PR review is nextGittifactsCost of an accepted result

Three proposals for what replaces line-by-line human review of agent PRs

'Human PR review is next' argues review shifts to requirements, acceptance testing and release ownership, with independent review models and guardrails; no numbers. 'Gittifacts' proposes humans approve intent while machines prove conformance, routing changes to auto-merge, quick or full review by test evidence and policy. A Life Engineered (Steve Huynh)Read

The Beautiful Mess (John Cutler)Read

The feature factory is a spectrum, not a binary, and outcome language can be a proxy too

Cutler is 'tired of outcomes vs outputs, projects vs products' binaries. 'Projects to products' led ZIRP-era companies to over-hire PMs and force premature convergence; features are a proxy teams use under low trust or many dependencies, and OKRs and metrics are proxies as well ('performative outcome-centricity'). Sometimes shipping a feature list is right.

What holds teams back is access to customers and data, autonomy, training, promotion structure and money; AI amplifies both good and bad habits. No quantitative claims. Full essay.

The Batch

Andrew Ng: LLMs are far stronger on verifiable tasks than on judgement tasks, and people overgeneralise

Reinforcement-learning gyms and automatic reward checks drive progress on verifiable work like code and math; judgement tasks (customer complaints, product features, strategy) have better and worse answers but no ground truth, so labels and rewards are harder. He expects humans to keep a context advantage on judgement for a long time and recalls overgeneralising from Deep Blue, Watson and AlphaGo. No numbers.

The email has no clean web link (tracking redirects only).

TLDR / TLDR AIDecoherenceThe expensive-loop problem

Two long reads on why change gets expensive: incompatible local models, and domains where feedback is scarce

'Decoherence' (48 minutes) defines it as the accumulation of locally valid but mutually incompatible models, so the cost of change becomes dominated by translating between them and grows with the rate of change. 'The expensive-loop problem' says high-value human work is in expensive-loop domains (real-world constraints and costs) that need theory building, proxy management and diagnosis under scarce feedback, which cheap-loop training does not teach.

Practical engineering reads, mostly from the TLDR family, with vendor-authored items marked.

Addy Osmani (Elevate) / TLDRRead

Agentic verification: quality comes from loops the agent can run itself

Verification, not code-writing, is the bottleneck. Osmani prefers end-to-end tests of a small set of high-value flows as ground truth, property-based tests (fast-check, Hypothesis, proptest) for what must never happen, and differential tests that use the old system as an oracle for migrations. Tests must be fast and deterministic or the agent learns to retry rather than fix; isolate flaky ones.

Write the check before the code, and have humans review evidence, not just diffs. No hard numbers. Free.

Neo Kim / System Design Onepaywalled previewPart 1Part 2

Why HTTP/2 still stalls on TCP packet loss, and why HTTP/3 and QUIC exist

Part 1 covers HTTP/1.x and connection setup: browsers typically open 6 parallel connections, TCP slow start begins at about 10 packets (roughly 14 KB) and doubles each round trip. Part 2 covers HTTP/2: Server Push was disabled in Chrome and removed in Firefox, Early Hints are used on only 2.9% of desktop pages, and TCP head-of-line blocking can make HTTP/2 on one connection sometimes worse than HTTP/1.1 on six on lossy networks.

Neo Kim / System Design Onepaywalled previewRead

Scaling an AI chat app to 1M users is mostly an inference-cost problem

On illustrative assumptions (8 LLM requests per conversation, 1,424 input and 300 output tokens each, GPT-5.4 at $2.50 / $15 per million), a conversation costs about $0.064, so the bill is about $193 a month at 100 daily users, $193,440 at 100,000 and $1.93M at 1M. Routing 70% of requests to GPT-5.4-mini cuts it about 49% to about $98,635; adding semantic caching (30% hit rate) and prompt trimming reaches about $48,000, a 75% cut.

The Pragmatic Engineer (Sam Newman excerpts)Read

Resilient AI-powered software: RPO and RTO of zero are impossible, and failover capacity may not exist when memory is scarce

Disaster-recovery patterns rise in cost from backup-and-restore through pilot light and warm failover to multisite active-active. GPU, CPU and memory shortages mean on-demand failover capacity may not exist, so reserve it, prioritise critical services or run redundant infrastructure. For AI features, plan for the model being down with fallback vendors, evals and dynamic timeouts, and make spend visible per person (James Stanier: a QA engineer burned over 1,000 euros of Claude Code in a week and more than doubled productivity).
