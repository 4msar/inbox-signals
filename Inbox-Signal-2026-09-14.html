The five, if you only read five

ranked

Picked for a consultancy engineering lead: things that change how you'd advise a client's AI spend, architect a review pipeline, or judge the next benchmark headline — not just what happened this week.

1
Pragmatic EngineerRead

GitHub PRs are up 5x in three years. Anthropic and OpenAI both told this reporter directly: low-risk changes now ship on AI approval alone.

Gergely Orosz's deep-dive names three emerging patterns replacing "a human reviews every diff." Most common: bots (CodeRabbit, Greptile, Claude Code Review, GitHub Copilot Code Review, and others) review the PR first and a human reviews the bot's review, not the code — Weaviate's CTO Etienne Dilocker runs a loop of adversarial-agent-review → human scope decision → agent implements feedback, with roughly 90% of that loop left to agents. Second: triage by blast radius — low-risk changes merge on AI approval alone, high-risk changes require a human, confirmed as the actual practice at both Anthropic and OpenAI directly by the author (Anthropic's Jarred Sumner: humans still merge even low-risk changes today, but the goal is eventually another Claude instance does it). Third: review the plan and the tests, not the generated code — one team spends hours front-loading a spec, then never reviews the resulting implementation at all, on the theory that the database schema is the one truly hard-to-reverse artifact worth scrutinizing.

The concrete before/after: five-person startup Duckbill Group adopted risk-based review plus tightened automated guardrails (nearly every lint rule enabled, an 85% test-coverage floor) to clear a 60-PR backlog — PRs merged per week rose 80→154 (+94%), merged-within-1-hour rose 28%→45%, merged-within-24-hours rose 76%→80%, and human-reviewed PRs had a 26-hour median merge time versus 1 hour for everything else. Worth raising with any client still routing 100% of PRs through a human: the bottleneck has moved, and pretending it hasn't is now measurably the slow path.

2
Pragmatic EngineerRead

Uber cut AI cost-per-session 52%, AT&T cut its AI bill 56% for a 2% quality drop — the industry is quietly routing off frontier models

Verified figures from the source: Uber cut cost-per-AI-request 34% and cost-per-AI-session 52% despite rising usage, via open-weight models on inference providers, weekly model benchmarking, cheaper subagent models, defaulting to "Medium" reasoning effort, and prompt caching — even its most expensive open-weight model runs $0.30/code review, still under the $0.50 cheapest frontier model charges (and well under the $2.50 priciest). Pinterest's CEO William Ready said open models post-trained on Pinterest's own data hit cost-per-transaction under 8% of comparable closed models — $100 of closed-model cost becomes roughly $8. AT&T cut its AI bill 56% moving from Claude/GPT to open models routed via LiteLLM, per The Information, for only a 2% quality drop — it still reserves frontier models for complex code generation and routes routine work like code-change summaries to cheap open models.

Databricks separately interviewed Stripe, Coinbase, Uber and Ramp and found open-model adoption is the single biggest AI-cost lever companies have pulled, ahead of smart routing — spend controls and context optimization help far less. And the pullback isn't just anecdotal: Ramp data (economist Ara Kharazian) shows AI spend at the top 1% of firms by AI usage declined 10% in August 2026. For a consultancy advising on AI cost: the lever with the best evidence behind it isn't prompt-engineering or caching, it's being willing to route off the flagship model in the first place.

The Batch / TLDR AI / Nextool AI / Fireship

GPT-6 Astra scored 62.7% on ARC-AGI-3 under the benchmark's own harness — and 99.9% under OpenAI's. Same model, same test, 37-point gap.

Astra launched at $10/$1(cached)/$12.50(cache-write)/$50 per million input/output tokens, and became the first model OpenAI says has crossed its "Critical" cybersecurity threshold under its own Preparedness Framework (trained on 100,000+ GPUs — OpenAI's largest run yet). Its headline number, though, is a lesson in reading benchmarks: on ARC-AGI-3, Astra scored 62.7% under ARC Prize's standard harness ($26,098 to run) but 99.9% under ARC Prize's own "Provider Adapter" harness ($18,817) — same model, same test, a 37-point swing from scaffolding alone. Fireship separately clocked the same effect on the public task set: OpenAI's Responses API harness lifted one model's score from 13.3% to 38.3% on identical questions. Astra's more checkable wins: it beat GPT-5.6 Sol on Agents' Last Exam (59.3% vs. 53.6%) using ~65% fewer tokens, scored 72.6% on OSWorld 2.0 in about half the time Sol needed, and placed a block in a bowl in 19 of 20 robot-arm trials (Claude Fable 5.1 managed 8 of 20) — though both models solved a harder puzzle-insertion task only twice.

Separately, an OpenAI-affiliated model (not Astra itself) claims to have produced a proof for the Navier-Stokes existence-and-smoothness Millennium Prize problem in 88 hours using roughly 10,000 agents and 130 billion tokens (an estimated $10-40M in compute), with Astra doing 17 hours of formal Lean verification on top — a genuinely striking result that's now tangled in a public priority dispute involving Anthropic-affiliated researchers and mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, with Terence Tao raising open-science-norms concerns. Worth the caution flag before repeating any of this to a client: this week's two biggest AI claims (an "AGI-tier" benchmark score and a 90-year-old unsolved math problem) both came with an asterisk attached by the people who made them.

The AI CornerRead

1% of OpenAI's and Anthropic's customers generate 80% of their enterprise revenue — and that split hasn't moved in three years

Per Ramp's own customer data (economist Ara Kharazian): the median business spends $11.95/employee/year on AI, versus the top 1% of spenders at $7,400/employee/year — a 600x gap, with the concentration itself unchanged for three years. Anthropic holds 43.5% of US paying-business AI subscriptions (still growing), OpenAI 39.7% (growth has nearly stalled), xAI 4% (fastest-growing). Cursor is the sharpest illustration of what customer concentration actually risks: two customers alone made up ~30% of Anthropic's ~$4B run-rate ($1.2B combined), and Cursor was on track for $1B+ of 2026 OpenAI revenue even though OpenAI models were only ~5% of its own traffic — then SpaceX's ~$60B acquisition of Cursor's parent in August led OpenAI to cut Cursor's access entirely, effective November 12, while Anthropic publicly pledged to expand Cursor's Claude compute instead. One customer's ownership change reshuffled two labs' revenue exposure overnight.

The bigger structural number: Anthropic and OpenAI's compute commitments now exceed $1.3 trillion in future hyperscaler/neocloud revenue combined (OpenAI alone projected at $750B+ through 2030; Anthropic has signed roughly $517B in agreements over 11 months). Only about $10B of Microsoft's $33.33B in FY2026 AI revenue came from Microsoft's own customers — the rest traces back to OpenAI's committed spend. None of this is urgent yet, the piece argues, because most of it is take-or-pay contracts not yet billed at real scale — that changes starting 2027, which is exactly the horizon worth flagging to any client treating "AI vendor" as a stable line item rather than a two-company bet.

ChecklyRead

Checkly rewrote a 92-million-message-a-day service from Node.js to Go using Claude Code — zero incidents, 70% fewer pods

A concrete production data point on agentic rewrites, not a benchmark: Checkly used Claude Code to rewrite a 13,000-line Node.js service handling 92 million messages a day into Go, steering the agent with a black-box test harness run continuously against production traffic rather than reviewing the generated code line by line. Result: the new service launched with zero incidents, running on 70% fewer pods and 60% fewer database sessions.

The one flagged wrinkle, worth keeping rather than editing out: partway through, the team discovered a mismatch between the test environment's queue configuration and production's — a reminder that a black-box harness only proves the harness is satisfied, not that the harness matches reality. As a template for how a consultancy might actually run an agentic migration for a client — test-harness-driven, production-traffic-validated, with the mismatch caught and fixed before launch rather than after — this is a more useful data point than another vendor's benchmark chart.

Leadership in TechRead

Rachel Laycock: code review used to do three jobs at once, and that model is breaking under AI-generated volume

Review has historically doubled as a quality gate, a teaching tool, and an architecture forum simultaneously — a model that held up at pre-AI PR volumes but breaks as volume climbs (GitHub data elsewhere this week shows PRs up 5x in three years). Laycock's proposed fix: move feedback earlier via pairing, collaborative design and automated checks, and reserve human review only for genuinely high-stakes changes by default rather than every diff — the same conclusion The Pragmatic Engineer's deep-dive (see the Five) reaches from a different angle, independently.

Engineering Leadershippart paywalled

Gregor Ojstersek's 4 concrete moves for growing past Senior Engineer — the free half of a paywalled piece

Ojstersek stagnated after his own promotion to Senior because he couldn't articulate a next-step goal to his manager. Four moves he now recommends: (1) tell your manager your career goals explicitly in 1:1s, sooner rather than later; (2) keep a running "brag list" of wins, since he notes a manager sees only about 40% of your daily work; (3) become the go-to person for one specific project, technology or domain — not three — via teaching, writing and helping others (he used this to go from Angular to React expert to team lead); (4) proactively propose and personally implement one impactful improvement rather than waiting to be assigned one — the single most promotion-correlated behavior in his own experience. (His companion piece on coaching-vs-mentoring frameworks this week is fully paywalled beyond the free preview.)

The real cost of software surfaces years later — patches, security fixes, engineer turnover on inherited code — not at build time, so a lower build cost changes less than it looks like. System design

9 items

ByteByteGo, System Design Nuggets and Neo Kim — course-marketing operations, but the free technical previews below stand on their own.

ByteByteGoRead

Four cache failure modes, four fixes — and why git revert conflicts with an untouched-looking commit

Thundering herd (mass simultaneous key expiry overloads the DB) is fixed with randomized TTL jitter; cache penetration (repeated lookups for keys that don't exist) is fixed by caching null values or a bloom filter; cache breakdown (one very hot key's expiry alone overloads the DB — hot keys often account for ~80% of queries) is fixed by simply not expiring hot keys; cache crashes are handled with circuit breakers or clustering. Separately: git revert creates a new commit undoing an earlier one without rewriting history, and conflicts when a later commit touched the same lines as the one being reverted — resolved like any merge conflict, by hand.

ByteByteGoRead

The routing math behind cutting LLM costs ~10x: send 85% of traffic to a cheap model, 10% to medium, 5% to the flagship

Worked example: a powerful model costs 1 unit/request, a small model 1/20th, a medium model 1/5th; routing 85% of traffic to small, 10% to medium, 5% to powerful yields an average cost of 0.1125 units — an ~89% cost reduction versus sending everything to the flagship. Techniques: router-model classification (a cheap model tags each prompt EASY/MEDIUM/HARD), cascading (try cheap first, escalate only on automated-validation failure — works for structured/testable output, poorly for subjective quality calls), and learned routing trained on historical eval data. Failure modes named explicitly: under-routing a hard task to a weak model, over-routing an easy one to an expensive model (erasing the savings), and prompt-injection attempts to manipulate the router itself.

ByteByteGoRead

A working error-handling taxonomy for LLM-powered applications

Splits failures into technical (network, timeout, 429, 5xx, auth, context-length overflow — conventional error handling already covers these) and semantic (malformed output, hallucinated facts, wrong tool call, business-rule violation — conventional handling doesn't). Recommends exponential backoff with jitter for transient errors, a fallback chain (primary model → smaller backup → cached response → human handoff, explicitly warning that using the same provider for primary and backup defeats the redundancy), a three-state circuit breaker for provider outages, and idempotency keys on tool calls like payments, where a lost confirmation on an actually-successful call risks a double-charge on blind retry.

ByteByteGoRead

How American Express's payment system fails on purpose, fast, rather than trying not to fail

Amex's cell-based architecture gives each "cell" a complete, independent copy of the payment stack with no synchronous cross-cell dependencies. On a mid-transaction failure, the orchestrator discards all partial work in the failing cell and restarts the whole transaction from scratch in a healthy cell — no state resumption — because a payment is sub-second, so redoing a few hundred milliseconds is cheaper than engineering cross-cell shared state. Static reference data (exchange rates, merchant codes) is pushed to every cell in advance; per-transaction data is instead routed to where it lives via a deliberately thin, stateless "Global Transaction Router" kept free of business logic to minimize its own failure surface.
