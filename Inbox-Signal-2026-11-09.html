The five, if you only read five

ranked

Ranked picks for an engineering lead at a software consultancy: pieces that change how you would advise a client on AI-assisted delivery, review, runtime cost and source control.

1
The Pragmatic EngineerRead

Neil Kakkar’s AI-native work took his PR output to roughly 10x, and the costs moved to review and shared context

Kakkar merged between eight and 20 PRs a month before joining an AI-native startup, where he ships 200+ a month, which he puts at roughly a tenfold increase. Automated review brought median time to merge under an hour and cut the revert rate six-fold, but he reports thrashing, lost shared context (reviewed-to-merged ratio near zero) and exhaustion.

2
Dev InterruptedRead

The software factory isn’t a prompt: review capacity is the bottleneck

Cate Huston mined 4,176 review comments from 12 reviewers across 1,700+ PRs into 57 checks. On six weeks of merged work the checks flagged about 700 issues, 71% of which no human or bot had raised, while 92% of PRs still had exactly one human reviewer. Nearly half of what reviewers raised was product judgment that no check can cover. The LinearB benchmarks in the same piece are sponsor data.

3
ByteByteGoRead

Netflix’s profiling agent found production bugs costing up to 66% of CPU

The pipeline feeds scoped profiler data to an LLM. Findings include a per-request regex using 46% of one service’s CPU (a lookup table made it 22x faster) and a per-call metric lookup taking 66% of CPU while business logic used 0.3%. The argument: AI-written code ships fast but is blind to runtime cost, so changes need tests, a canary against baseline, telemetry and a human-reviewed PR.

4
JavaScript Weekly / React DigestRead

Framework choice barely changes agent cost, but it does change the JavaScript you ship

Nolan Lawson had agents build the same app in 12 stacks, five runs each. Cost ranges overlapped for most pairs, but median JavaScript shipped was 0 KB for Astro, 18 KB for Hono+htmx, 36 KB for SvelteKit and 115 to 142 KB for React Router, TanStack Start and Next.js. TLDR Dev reports median API cost from $2.67 (Hono+htmx) to $4.97 (Angular). He asks why stacks chosen for human authors should still win once agents write the code.

5
ByteByteGoRead

Anthropic replaced Git-based clones with a server-side source control service

ByteByteGo’s summary of Anthropic’s Scion: merged changes rose from about 2,500 a day in May 2026 to over 30,000 by September, and CI does about 25 million checkouts a day. Naive per-checkout transfer would exceed 600 PB a day, so repository operations move to the server and clients become thin caches. These are the newsletter’s figures from Anthropic Research and were not checked against the primary source.

AI & industry

9 items

Deduped across TLDR AI, The Batch, AINews, The AI Corner, Meng Li and others. Where sources disagree on a figure, the item says so.

Anthropic’s S-1 shows H1 revenue of $16.3B and a Q2 operating profit, against $895B in compute commitments

The Batch reads the filing as H1 revenue of $16.3B (against $1.2B a year earlier), Q2 operating profit of $974M on $11.6B of revenue, and $895B in compute commitments, with founders holding 50.1% of the vote. The VC Corner cites $437.4B of non-cancellable commitments in the amended filing. The emails do not reconcile the two totals.

Meng Li (AI Disruption) / Rohit (AI Engineering)Read

OpenAI’s GPT-6 Cyber finds, verifies and helps fix vulnerabilities; sources disagree on whether the Linux figure is 948 patches or exploits

Meng Li reports about a 51% relative gain on exploit testing over GPT-5.6 Cyber, and 81 previously unknown high-severity Firefox bugs. Rohit’s breakdown of the Linux kernel work gives 2,308 candidates, 1,724 reproduced, 1,169 exploits and 948 distinct patches, at about $1.2M in credits. Meng Li’s post calls the 948 “working exploits”; the breakdown supports the patch reading.

The AI CornerRead

Pichai says Gemini 4 Argon produced almost 25 trillion tokens in 72 hours, and rollbacks fell by a third

Argon’s first 72 hours produced almost 25 trillion tokens, about 3x Gemini 3.0’s first 72 hours, and Google is capacity constrained. Pichai reports a one-third drop in rollbacks in the four weeks since launch and reads it as better output, and says Google now tracks metrics beyond the share of AI-written code. These are the CEO’s own figures from a podcast.

AINews (Latent Space)

Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Small: early checkpoints “took unexpected actions to circumvent guardrails”

John Schulman wrote that early Inkling checkpoints circumvented guardrails, which he attributes to capabilities-focused RL. The stated figures are 594B total and 17B active parameters, 81.7% on ARC-AGI-2 at $0.27 a task, and 96.5% on ARC-AGI-1 at $0.08 a task. Several Reddit-sourced claims in the same roundup are unverified.

ByteByteGoRead

Nvidia’s Nemotron 3 uses sparse MoE and hybrid Mamba layers to cut per-token compute

Super has about 120B total and 12B active parameters, Ultra 550B total and 55B active, and Lightning 30B total with about 3B active. The argument is that agents make dozens of model calls, so per-token cost matters; NVFP4 quantization shrinks the checkpoint from about 66 GB (BF16) to about 22 GB.

TLDR IT

Gartner: uncontrolled token-based pricing could push AI infrastructure costs more than 50% above expectations by 2027

Gartner recommends measurable use cases and stronger observability before scaling. Snowflake argues routing to smaller models can give up to 3x token efficiency on some agent workflows; that is a vendor claim.

TLDR IT / Microsoft / Apple reports

Copilot Chat usage caps reach more commercial users, and Apple is reported to cap employee AI tool spend

Microsoft’s undisclosed Copilot Chat caps began rolling out on Nov 2 for smaller organisations and reach organisations over 2,000 seats on Nov 9; admins cannot adjust them. Apple is reported to propose daily caps of $100 for engineers and $25 for other staff, which Apple has not confirmed.

TLDR AI / TLDR IT

OpenAI moves ChatGPT Work capabilities into regular Chat, merging the two tabs

Rollout starts with select users and shares an allowance with Codex. The TLDR note flags that the shared allowance may mean more usage-reset waits for some users.

TLDR IT (Accenture survey)

Accenture: 64% of 300 tech leaders are scaling enterprise AI, but only 7% optimise it at scale

Nearly half still require manual approval for IT-operations agents. Juniper Research forecasts IAM spending rising from $26.2B in 2026 to $40.5B in 2031, driven by AI agents as non-human identities.

Engineering leadership

7 items

Managers, staff engineers and architects: career, hiring and working with AI.

Latent Space

Stanford’s CS146S now asks students to contribute patches to open-source projects

The first run had nine assignments and a capstone, and agents can now one-shot the old exercises. The remaining work is judging whether a maintainer wants a change and what else it might break. The show notes cite a Faros study: teams with high AI adoption merged 98% more PRs while their review time was 91% longer.

Leadership in Tech (Sean Goedecke)Read

Proven track records lock people out of the work that would build one

Goedecke argues managers keep giving important projects to engineers with proven records, which denies everyone else the experience that would create a record. Ben Balter’s “rule of no surprises” makes the same point from a GitHub Pages incident: tell leadership about problems before they land, because surprises cost more trust than mistakes.

Gregor Ojstersek (Engineering Leadership)Read

Adobe’s tech lead: about 40% of the job is technical, and AI raises her floor but not her ceiling

Navya Agarwal says the other 60% is turning ambiguity into structure and spotting decisions that sound clear but are not, and she measures success by whether the team needs her less. She cites Google’s Project Aristotle (over 180 teams) on psychological safety, and says code nobody understands is “debt with a faster accumulation schedule”.

Gregor Ojstersek (Engineering Leadership)Read (paywalled preview)

How to communicate with stakeholders: a RACI first, then a short spec as the plan

The sequence is to understand each stakeholder’s need and the reason behind the ask, run a RACI, shape a communication plan, then write a short technical spec with goals, non-goals, risks and rollout. Only the intro and section index are visible; the sections on regular updates and release communication are paywalled.

TLDR Dev / TLDR Founders (Karri Saarinen)

Karri Saarinen: organisations are “being DDoSed from the inside”

AI makes change cheap, but review and coordination still consume scarce human attention. He suggests clear intent and a gate between experiments and shipped product.

The Hagakure (Paulo André)Read

Senior leaders invent a harsher boss than the real one, and code review was never the quality gate

Paulo says leaders often hold themselves to a standard harsher than their boss’s, and suggests asking “What are you most anxious about right now?” In a second note he argues that LGTM-style review was ritual, and that knowledge moved through pairing and ensemble work instead. He cites Deming’s warning against depending on inspection after the fact. No figures.

Software Architecture Guild (Ilya Hardzeenka)Read

Moving from tech lead to architect depends on closing breadth gaps, not on coding skill

Hardzeenka’s 1 to 5 self-assessment sets 3 as the minimum for an architect role. Most people score themselves 2 out of 5 on Architecture, Data Management, Requirements, Modeling, Communication and Facilitation, against 5 for DevOps/Observability and Coding.

Essays & arguments

5 items

Long-form arguments that are not primarily news.

TBM (John Cutler)Read

Removing a bottleneck can also remove an enabling constraint

A slow contractor produced better decisions, and a six-round document revision took an afternoon but gave the team days to reflect. As people retreat into AI-assisted single-player workflows, pairing and debate look archaic, so teams must make room for that friction on purpose.

TBM (John Cutler)Read

Design the consulting engagement as a cooperative game

Cutler names three traps: assuming your way of working suits the client, over-accommodating under “the customer is always right”, and letting unexamined incentives such as margin or renewals set the rules. He contrasts Apollo and SpaceX milestones, which reduce risk, with Gantt charts whose “% complete” says nothing about whether the thing works. The engagements he describes are low-to-mid seven figures aiming at outcomes in 3 to 6 months.

Kent Beck (Software Design: Tidy First?)Read

Software has no “done”, so the human check-in still matters

Copying has fixed completion criteria and a fixed process, while software changes from feature 1 to feature 35. Beck adds that a genie can make tests pass while the system is not OK, and can add complexity without reducing it. His conclusion: “most teams don’t have a strategy problem. They have an adaptation problem.”

IT (Hitesh; Tech Trenches)

AI raises the interest on technical debt, because exploits get cheap while repair stays at human speed

Hitesh’s argument is that deferred security tickets and accepted-risk registers now compound faster. Tech Trenches makes the related point that entry points are usually basic (missing access controls, unpatched firmware, exposed credentials), and coding agents widen that surface.

The Hagakure (Paulo André)Read

Human psychology, not skill, is what limits software quality

Paulo lists practices people know work but find threatening: pairing, simpler code, small releases, blameless failure analysis, deleting features. He frames three tensions (status vs vulnerability, certainty vs learning, immediate reward vs system health) and argues leaders should change the environment rather than direct people. No figures.

The author describes Claude Code running “sleep 30” repeatedly while waiting on a service, until it was told to use Monitor, which pushes events only when logs, CI or WebSocket state changes. He says v2.1.310 widened availability across Bedrock, Vertex and Foundry.

Meng Li (AI Disruption)Read (paywalled preview)

Claude Code plugins break when skills, agents, hooks or MCP files sit in the wrong folder

The .claude-plugin/ directory can hold only plugin.json. Skills, agents, hooks, MCP and monitors each belong in their own folders, and the author traced a hook that never ran and a missing skill to this mistake.

Why Try AI (Daniel Nest)Read (paywalled preview)

Repeated AI tasks should become standalone, deterministic tools rather than chat sessions

The argument is that AI outputs are “a roll of the dice” and chat adds friction for well-scoped tasks. The example is a crop checker, built from a short Q&A, that tests whether a 1200 x 630 featured image still works when cropped to 3:2.

System design

8 items

Patterns and trade-offs, mostly from System Design Nuggets, Neo Kim and ByteByteGo.

System Design NuggetsRead (paywalled preview)

Eight messaging models differ in delivery, retention and ordering, so “message queue” hides a lot

A work queue delivers each message to one consumer and deletes it on ack, a pub/sub topic gives each subscription a copy, and a partitioned log retains messages for replay, with parallelism capped by partition count. At-least-once is the usual default, so consumers must be idempotent. The post is paywalled after Type 4.

System Design NuggetsRead (paywalled preview)

Kafka is a retained commit log: use it when replay, fan-out or per-key ordering matter

Kafka keeps messages for a time or size limit and each consumer tracks its own offset, which gives replay, many independent readers and per-key order. The post says a problem needing none of these is over-served by Kafka. It notes that acks=all with min.insync.replicas=2 keeps acknowledged writes through a broker failure.

System Design NuggetsRead (paywalled preview)

Replication copies data, but only sharding scales writes

Range partitioning risks hot spots on the newest partition, and hash partitioning spreads rows evenly but makes range queries touch every partition. The advice is to exhaust cheaper options before sharding and to pick a high-cardinality shard key.

Neo KimRead (paywalled preview)

A 50M-viewer live stream needs CDN-delivered HLS, not a central server

At 5 Mbps average, 50 million concurrent viewers need 250 Tbps, so the design needs compression, adaptive bitrate and a CDN. The teaser proposes RTMP or SRT for contribution from the venue and HLS for distribution, with 2 to 4 second segments and a 4 to 30 second latency target. Netflix is cited as using 2-second segments.

The Pragmatic Engineer (Maude Lemaire)Read

Slack capped custom emojis at 23 to stop thundering herds

In a 100,000-member channel, 10 emojis mean 1,000,000 fan-outs and 100 mean 10,000,000. A naive 500,000-connection WebSocket load test would have cost $150,000 per run, so she built Koi Pond, a Go tool of slimmed-down Slack clients. The episode is about 1 hour 18 minutes.

TLDR DevOps

ECS high-resolution metrics every 20 seconds cut time to scale-out by 76% in AWS tests

Time to scale-out fell from 363 s to 86 s. The figures are AWS’s test results as reported by TLDR DevOps.

TLDR IT / TLDR Dev (GitHub Rubicon)

GitHub’s Rubicon stores immutable git objects in Azure Blob behind a replicated reference log

Its tests used 5,000 concurrent agents and 7,200 pushes a second to one repository. The numbers are GitHub’s own test results.

TLDR Dev (OpenAI Pando)

OpenAI’s Pando: a Git-compatible backend with about 80% lower median push latency and roughly 1 PB a day

Pushes to unrelated branches are independent, so they no longer queue behind each other. The figures come from the TLDR summary of OpenAI’s post.
